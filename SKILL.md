# Research family history

Start with something the family remembers and turn it into two questions. *Which record would identify this person or connect these two people? Which record might tell us something about the life they led?* Pursue both. A branch can yield a remarkable story while its next ancestor remains unknown.

For a single record or a focused question, go straight to the relevant guide and deliver the requested reading, answer or map.

## Start a case

1. Identify the starting person, approximate dates, places, relatives and remembered wording. Attribute oral history to the person who supplied it. Preserve uncertain spellings, nicknames and places as search terms. Record later family confirmations with their date and scope.
2. Follow the researcher's preferences for branches, depth, sources, contact, access and sharing. For an ongoing project, put these decisions in its README. A useful starting scope is online records, no outreach, and one priority branch. When an undigitized item matters, give the researcher the archive, collection and item to request. Keep living people's exact dates, addresses, identifying documents and family testimony private unless the researcher authorizes sharing them.
3. Reuse the project's archive, identifiers and confidence terms. For a new multi-session project, copy the [casebook](assets/casebook.md) into the research folder and fill in the research agreement. Its source register, claim ledger and single retrieval queue can grow into separate files as needed. A [fictional worked case](references/worked-case.md) shows what a filled entry, a defensible relationship and a readable family story look like together.

For an unfamiliar repository, choose a collection by its place, years and record type using the [source guide](references/source-guide.md).

## Work a branch

1. Choose the first unresolved relationship working backward from an identified person. Follow the [kinship and evidence guide](references/kinship-and-evidence.md). For a possible common ancestor, build both paths independently. Track a non-biological relative under the relationship the evidence supports.
2. Pick one concrete clue about an identified person and follow it into the records their activity generated. Use the [biographical search guide](references/biographical-search.md) for newspapers, education, photographs, occupations, awards and service. Follow incidental mentions of relatives or neighbors when they help answer a question.
3. Open the record image, read its surroundings and save the best permitted copy with a citation. For testimony, old handwriting or a journey, use the [manuscript and route guide](references/manuscripts-and-routes.md). Connect each conclusion to the individual page, entry or passage supporting it.
4. If a search stalls, vary the name, jurisdiction, collection or type of record. If access stalls, retry later and keep the item in the shared queue. Continue an independent lead. Choose the next search by what it could establish, even when a more famous or earlier ancestor looks promising.

For a deep search, alternate the two kinds of work. Follow the strongest parentage question back one generation, then revisit a known relative in a collection the branch has just uncovered. Search a whole yearbook or trade journal for siblings, partners, nicknames and hometowns. Record what the caption or entry establishes about each person and date any inferred connection separately.

When independent questions can progress at the same time and delegation is authorized, use the [subagent field guide](references/subagent-research.md). It includes an assignment you can copy, the evidence to request back, and how to reconcile overlapping findings and downloads. Keep a single researcher on a chain where the next search depends on identifying the previous person.

## Deliver a result someone can check

Lead with the relationship or discovery, quote or describe the revealing detail, and link directly to its evidence. State what remains uncertain, the best competing explanation where relevant, and the specific record that could distinguish them. Update the tree, log and retrieval queue together in ongoing projects. For an explorer, timeline or chat map, use the [presentation guide](references/presenting-the-family.md).

Close a research pass when the agreed question has a supported answer or the next decision depends on a specific unavailable record after checking credible alternatives. Give the reader the strongest supported generation on each requested line, even if an older candidate has an enticing biography. For a continuing archive, leave the exact volume, years, search terms or next page that will let someone pick up the work.
