# Read a manuscript and map a narrative

## Transcribe the record

Inspect every image in order, including covers, endorsements, continuation pages and later correspondence. Keep the archive's image number alongside any handwritten page number. Identify the document, date, writer or clerk, speaker and purpose. A pension file can hold testimony, affidavits from neighbors and a government summary written a century later.

Read from the scan. Enlarge or rotate a working view, then compare uncertain letterforms against repeated names or words in the same hand. OCR and published transcriptions can suggest readings. Return to the original for every uncertain name, date and place. If the user asks for a transcription, deliver the text page by page. Preserve spelling, meaningful deletions, additions and uncertainties. Explain conventions once, for example `[illegible]`, `[Brook?]` and `[inserted above: …]`. Supply normalized names and editorial commentary separately. Indicate missing or duplicated pages.

## Turn testimony into events

1. Extract an event with its speaker, actor, action, date as written, place as written and page locator. Separate “I marched,” “we heard,” and “my father told me.” A battle mentioned to date an episode does not establish that the speaker fought there.
2. Keep exact dates, intervals, sequences and approximate periods distinct. Carry “about,” “after,” and date ranges through to the timeline. For each location, preserve the historical name and jurisdiction, add a supported modern identification, and record the basis for it. Two counties with the same name may represent two migrations.
3. Number the stops in the order supported by the narrative. Label inferred sequence, uncertain locations and gaps. Draw a connecting line only when the document supports movement between the places, and explain when its course is schematic. An in-chat map can be a simple itinerary with page references. A reader should be able to check it without opening an interactive map.

## A fictional reading

A deposition reads, “I left the north mill after the frost. My brother told me the winter road had washed out.” The witness places themself at the mill and attributes the road report to a brother. Preserve “after the frost” as a relative date. The statement gives neither a departure day nor a route. If the bundle contains a later clerk’s list of workers with the same surname, log those names as leads. An appointment book or payroll keyed to the correct mill could identify the family and narrow the season.

If a place appears under two jurisdictions during one lifetime, consult a period map or boundary chronology before treating the change as a move. Record the source for that decision separately from the testimony. [Newberry Library’s historical county boundaries](https://publications.newberry.org/ahcb/)
